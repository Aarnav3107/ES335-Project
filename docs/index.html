What makes a prediction good, the model or the data behind it?

When people talk about machine learning they usually mean the models, and the data work gets treated like a boring side job you rush through before the real fun starts. In this project we took three real tabular datasets and kept asking the same question: how much of a good prediction comes from cleaning the data and engineering better features, and how much comes from picking a stronger model?

Start with the NYC Taxi case study → Read the project summary

Our short answer after three experiments: it depends on the problem, but in a way you can measure. In NYC Taxi the data was hiding the signal, in Seoul Bikes the way we evaluated decided the winner, and in Football xG knowing the game let a simple model catch up with a complex one.

Where does predictive performance actually come from?

We didn’t want to start by announcing that data preparation always wins. So in every case study we change the data representation and the model separately, watch how the held-out scores move in each case, and say clearly where the evidence supports our hypothesis and where it doesn’t.

The research behind it: the project is inspired by A Data-Centric Perspective on Evaluating Machine Learning Models for Tabular Data (NeurIPS 2024). It is our own investigation built around the paper’s idea, and not a full reproduction of it. Read the source paper ↗

One question, answered three different ways

We think the balance between better data and better models depends on the problem itself, and the three case studies are our evidence for that.

Each case study walks you through the question, the decisions we made about the data, the experiments we actually ran, and what those experiments can and can’t tell us. The interactive parts are there so that you can check the evidence yourself instead of just taking our word for it.

The NeurIPS 2024 paper that inspired this project argues that how a tabular dataset is prepared belongs in the evaluation just as much as the choice of model, and that comparisons between models can look very different once the data is handled with care. NYC Taxi and Football xG are our evidence for the first half of that: a cleaned dataset and a well-chosen feature did more for Linear Regression than anything else, and six football features closed the gap between Logistic Regression and XGBoost.

Seoul Bikes adds something we didn’t expect when we started. Before you can say whether better data or a better model helps more, you have to decide what “generalise” means for your problem, because the same models with the same settings swapped places when we changed the split. So the order we would now recommend is to settle the evaluation first, then look hard at the data, and only then argue about the model.

Five lessons we learned the hard way

  1. Check what is known at prediction time. The dropoff time, the metered distance and anything that happens after a shot all look like great features, and every one of them would be cheating.
  2. Look at the data before reaching for a stronger model. In NYC a perfectly good distance feature did nothing until we found the zero coordinates, the negative durations and the meters left running for a day.
  3. Split by the natural unit of the problem. Hours from the same day and shots from the same match share conditions, so they have to stay together, the way our Seoul day split and xG match split keep them.
  4. Write down what counts as success before you run. In xG we fixed the rule for “competitive” before the rematch, so we couldn’t move the goalposts after seeing the numbers.
  5. Don’t trust the result of a single split. Seoul showed us how much one split can change the story, and in xG we repeated everything on ten match splits before calling the result level.

What this project can’t tell you

  • Each experiment covers a narrow slice of time: one month of taxi trips, one year of bike rentals and one season of football. None of them is tested on a later period or a different city or league.
  • Most results come from a single split and a single seed. Only Football xG repeats its split, so the NYC and Seoul numbers have no measure of how much they would vary.
  • NYC Taxi uses random splits rather than the time-based split we originally planned, and its boosting model was still improving when it hit the 2,000-round cap. A time-based split is the first thing we would add next.
  • None of the stronger models went through a proper tuning study, so a tuned XGBoost or boosting model might do better than the numbers shown here.
  • Our conclusions are about these three problems. We think the pattern is useful, but three datasets are not enough to turn it into a general rule.

Everything you need to follow our steps

  • Notebooks: the final Kaggle notebooks with their saved outputs, for NYC Taxi, Seoul Bikes and Football xG.
  • Data: the datasets aren’t redistributed here. NYC Taxi and Seoul Bikes come from Kaggle (NYC Yellow Taxi ↗, Seoul Bike Sharing ↗), and the football shots come from StatsBomb Open Data ↗, built into a table by a separate Kaggle notebook.
  • The numbers on this site: every score is taken from a saved notebook output, and the Football xG and Seoul timeline data are generated by scripts in the tools folder, which check their output against the notebooks.
  • Splits and seeds: every split uses random_state=42, and the exact split code is shown on each case-study page.
